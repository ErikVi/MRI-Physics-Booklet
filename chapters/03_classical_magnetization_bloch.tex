\chapter{Classical Magnetization and the Bloch Equations}
\label{ch:03}

A measured transverse signal can decrease because individual moments lose
coherence, because coherent subensembles cancel, because spins leave the
observed volume, or because the receiver weights change. A useful dynamical
model distinguishes these mechanisms before assigning a relaxation time.
The Bloch equations combine the exact first-moment torque law with a
phenomenological description of equilibration \cite{bloch1946}.
Their simplicity is valuable precisely when the assumptions of that
reduction are made explicit.

\section{From magnetic torque to ensemble dynamics}
\label{sec:03:from-magnetic-torque-to-ensemble-dynamics}
\subsection{Torque equation and conserved magnitude}
From \cref{eq:02:ehrenfest}, equivalent independent spins obey
$\dot{\vect M}=\gamma\vect M\times\vect B$.
The field may depend on time, but at each instant
$\vect M\cdot\dot{\vect M}=0$, so torque preserves $\norm{\vect M}$.
For a constant field it also preserves $\vect M\cdot\vect B$.
Neither loss of transverse magnitude nor recovery of longitudinal
polarization follows from torque alone.

A spatially resolved magnetization is a local ensemble average.
Taking a spatial average of the torque equation is not generally the same
as evolving the average in the average field:
$\langle\vect M\times\vect B\rangle$ can differ from
$\langle\vect M\rangle\times\langle\vect B\rangle$.
The missing correlation is exactly what makes intravoxel dephasing
possible.

\subsection{Equilibrium magnetization and phenomenological relaxation}
For a dominant static field along $z$, suppose small departures from
longitudinal equilibrium relax at rate $R_1=1/T_1$, and transverse
first moments relax isotropically at rate $R_2=1/T_2$.
Then
\begin{equation}
 \dot{\vect M}
 =\gamma\vect M\times\vect B
 -R_2M_x\vect e_x-R_2M_y\vect e_y
 -R_1(M_z-M_0)\vect e_z .
 \label{eq:03:bloch}
\end{equation}
The source $R_1M_0$ is essential: replacing the last term by $-R_1M_z$
would equilibrate to zero polarization at finite field.
These are relaxation toward the dominant-field equilibrium axes, not
an isotropic shrinking of a vector toward its instantaneous effective
RF field.

The rates can be motivated by a bath with short memory compared with
the observed dynamics, weak coupling and approximately stationary
fluctuation statistics. Longitudinal recovery represents exchange of
Zeeman energy; transverse decay also responds to phase perturbations.
The quantum rate model in \cref{ch:02} illustrates why one cannot choose
arbitrary microscopic $T_1,T_2$ independently.

\subsection{Assumptions of the single-pool Bloch model}
Equation \eqref{eq:03:bloch} assumes that one set of local rates and one
equilibrium magnetization characterize the observed pool.
It neglects explicit spin-spin correlations, chemical exchange, transport
and memory. These can be added, but they are not already encoded by
renaming every observed decay constant $T_2$.

RF excitation can rotate the vector far from equilibrium while the
linear relaxation law remains a useful approximation for weakly polarized
nuclear ensembles. This is different from claiming that the RF dynamics
themselves are a small perturbation. Conversely, multi-exponential
tissue signals or a broad distribution of environments may not admit
one unique relaxation time.

\section{Solutions in a static field}
\label{sec:03:solutions-in-a-static-field}
\subsection{Longitudinal recovery and inversion}
With no transverse RF, separation gives
\begin{equation}
 M_z(t)=M_0+[M_z(0)-M_0]\ee^{-t/T_1}.
 \label{eq:03:recovery}
\end{equation}
An ideal saturation preparation has $M_z(0)=0$ and recovers as
$M_0(1-\ee^{-t/T_1})$.
An ideal inversion from equilibrium has $M_z(0)=-M_0$ and recovers as
$M_0(1-2\ee^{-t/T_1})$. Its null occurs at $t=T_1\ln2$.
This null formula requires fully recovered initial magnetization and
perfect inversion; repeated readouts and finite repetition times
alter the initial condition.

If the preparation gives $M_z(0)=-\eta M_0$, the null is
$T_1\ln(1+\eta)$ for $\eta>0$.
If an experiment reports only magnitude, negative and positive
longitudinal values can lead to the same magnitude after a fixed readout.
The sign information is recoverable only with an appropriate phase
reference or signal model.

\subsection{Complex transverse solution and phase}
Writing $M_+=M_x+\ii M_y$ combines the transverse equations:
\[
 \dot M_+=-(R_2+\ii\omega_0)M_+,\qquad
 M_+(t)=M_+(0)\ee^{-R_2t}\ee^{-\ii\omega_0t}.
\]
In rotating coordinates replace $\omega_0$ by $\Delta\omega$.
For time-varying longitudinal offset with constant $R_2$,
\begin{equation}
 M_+(t)=M_+(0)\ee^{-R_2t}
       \exp\left[-\ii\int_0^t\Delta\omega(u)\dd u\right].
 \label{eq:03:free}
\end{equation}
This scalar equation commutes with itself at different times.
It does not justify the same replacement of time ordering for RF fields
whose axes vary.

\subsection{Affine propagators and piecewise-constant fields}
In Cartesian components \cref{eq:03:bloch} is
$\dot{\vect M}=\mat A\vect M+\vect b$, with
\begin{equation}
 \mat A=\begin{pmatrix}
 -R_2&\gamma B_z&-\gamma B_y\\
 -\gamma B_z&-R_2&\gamma B_x\\
 \gamma B_y&-\gamma B_x&-R_1
 \end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \vect b=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\R_1M_0\end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:03:generator}
\end{equation}
Variation of constants gives
\[
 \vect M(t)=\ee^{t\mat A}\vect M(0)+
             \int_0^t\ee^{(t-u)\mat A}\vect b\dd u.
\]
If $\mat A$ is invertible, the integral is
$\mat A^{-1}(\ee^{t\mat A}-\mat I)\vect b$.
A numerically safer formulation valid also when rates vanish is
\begin{equation}
 \begin{pmatrix}\vect M(t)\\1\end{pmatrix}
 =\exp\left[t\begin{pmatrix}\mat A&\vect b\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\right]
  \begin{pmatrix}\vect M(0)\\1\end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:03:affine}
\end{equation}
Successive time intervals multiply these propagators in chronological
order, with the earliest acting on the right. The augmented coordinate
is a bookkeeping device for recovery, not a fourth magnetization component.

\section{Inhomogeneous ensembles}
\label{sec:03:inhomogeneous-ensembles}
\subsection{Frequency distributions and free induction decay}
Let $P(\Delta\omega)$ be a normalized offset density, with common
initial transverse magnetization and common $T_2$. The ensemble signal is
\begin{equation}
 \overline M_+(t)=M_+(0)\ee^{-t/T_2}
       \int P(\Delta\omega)\ee^{-\ii\Delta\omega t}\dd\Delta\omega .
 \label{eq:03:ensemble}
\end{equation}
The integral is the characteristic function of the offset distribution.
For a Gaussian centered at $\overline\omega$ with standard deviation
$\sigma_\omega$, it is
$\ee^{-\ii\overline\omega t}\ee^{-\sigma_\omega^2t^2/2}$.
For a Lorentzian with angular half-width $a$, it is
$\ee^{-\ii\overline\omega t}\ee^{-a\abs t}$.
For a uniform density over $[-\Omega,\Omega]$, it is
$\sin(\Omega t)/(\Omega t)$.
A uniform field gradient across a uniformly weighted slab generates the
last case. Zeros and sign changes of an FID can arise from coherent
cancellation rather than an oscillating relaxation rate.

\subsection{T2, T2-star and reversible broadening}
For Lorentzian static broadening and $t\geq0$, the envelope is exponential
with $R_2^*=R_2+a$. The frequently used decomposition
$R_2^*=R_2+R_2'$ is therefore exact for that specific broadening model.
It is not a theorem for an arbitrary voxel's frequency distribution.

Consider a phase-zero ideal $\pi$ pulse at time $\tau$.
It maps $M_+\mapsto M_+^*$ and $M_z\mapsto-M_z$.
For a static offset, free evolution before and after gives
\begin{equation}
 M_+(t)=M_+^*(0)\ee^{-t/T_2}
       \ee^{-\ii\Delta\omega(t-2\tau)},\qquad t>\tau .
 \label{eq:03:echo}
\end{equation}
The ensemble rephases at $2\tau$ while the factor $\ee^{-2\tau/T_2}$
remains. This is the essential refocusing mechanism behind spin echoes
\cite{hahn1950}. The pulse does not undo random motion through a gradient,
RF errors, exchange, or arbitrary rapid environmental fluctuations.

\subsection{Limits of exponential effective relaxation}
For the Gaussian case, the log-envelope slope is
$-\dd\ln\abs{\overline M_+}/\dd t=R_2+\sigma_\omega^2t$.
A fitted exponential rate consequently depends on the observation window.
A multi-pool signal
$S(t)=\sum_a A_a\ee^{-R_{2a}t}$ also has a time-dependent slope,
even without static frequency dispersion.

A useful extension treats $\delta\omega(t)$ as a stationary zero-mean
Gaussian random process with correlation
$C(u-v)=\E[\delta\omega(u)\delta\omega(v)]$.
For a pulse-dependent toggling sign $y(u)=\pm1$,
$\Phi=\int_0^t y(u)\delta\omega(u)\dd u$ is Gaussian, so
\[
 \E[\ee^{-\ii\Phi}]
 =\exp\left[-\frac12\int_0^t\!\!\int_0^t
                  y(u)y(v)C(u-v)\dd u\dd v\right].
\]
A constant offset is canceled when $\int y=0$.
Very short-correlated fluctuations generally are not.
The expression explains why an echo is a filter of fluctuation timescales,
not a universal reversal of every mechanism called dephasing.

\section{Driven and time-dependent dynamics}
\label{sec:03:driven-and-time-dependent-dynamics}
\subsection{Constant effective-field solution}
In rotating coordinates, let the RF field be along $x$ with
$\omega_1=\gamma B_a$ and detuning $\Delta\omega$.
Without relaxation the vector rotates about
$\vect n=(\omega_1,0,\Delta\omega)/\Omega$ at angle $-\Omega t$,
where $\Omega=(\omega_1^2+\Delta\omega^2)^{1/2}$.
Rodrigues' identity gives
\[
 \vect M(t)=\vect M(0)\cos\Omega t
 -(\vect n\times\vect M(0))\sin\Omega t
 +\vect n[\vect n\cdot\vect M(0)](1-\cos\Omega t).
\]
With relaxation use \cref{eq:03:affine}. It remains exact for constant
coefficients even when relaxation and RF rotations do not commute.

The continuous-drive steady state follows by setting derivatives to zero:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
 M_z^{\rm ss}&=\frac{M_0(1+\Delta\omega^2T_2^2)}
 {1+\Delta\omega^2T_2^2+\omega_1^2T_1T_2},\\
 M_y^{\rm ss}&=\frac{M_0\omega_1T_2}
 {1+\Delta\omega^2T_2^2+\omega_1^2T_1T_2},\qquad
 M_x^{\rm ss}=\Delta\omega T_2M_y^{\rm ss}.
 \end{split}
 \label{eq:03:cw}
\end{equation}
First use $M_x=\Delta\omega T_2M_y$, then
$M_y=\omega_1T_2M_z/(1+\Delta\omega^2T_2^2)$, and finally longitudinal
balance. Large resonant drive saturates $M_z$ rather than aligning it
with the effective field. Off-resonance reduces saturation.

\subsection{Numerical integration and operator splitting}
Piecewise-constant waveform intervals can be propagated by augmented
matrix exponentials. For smooth waveforms, a midpoint approximation to
the generator is second order when the waveform is sufficiently resolved.
Alternatively, split torque and relaxation:
$\mathcal P(h)\approx\mathcal R(h/2)\mathcal U(h)\mathcal R(h/2)$,
where $\mathcal U$ is an exact rotation and $\mathcal R$ exact affine
relaxation. This symmetric splitting has local error $O(h^3)$,
global error $O(h^2)$ for smooth bounded generators.
Applying a whole relaxation step and then a whole RF step is generally
only first order.

The laboratory carrier may require extremely small integration steps.
A valid rotating-wave model removes this fast scale, leaving RF envelope,
detuning and gradient timescales. Removing the carrier is a physical
approximation for a linear RF drive; numerical speed does not establish
its validity.

\subsection{Accuracy, stability and conservation checks}
A forward-Euler precession update multiplies a transverse amplitude by
$1-\ii\omega h$, whose magnitude exceeds one.
It artificially increases magnetization even if $h$ is small.
An exact rotation preserves length to roundoff and is a more informative
baseline than merely observing that a curve looks smooth.

A simulator should reproduce recovery, free precession, ideal inversion
and static-offset refocusing, conserve length without relaxation, and
converge under step halving. Tests must include noncommuting pulse axes
and finite detuning. A waveform raster is an input representation, not
automatically a sufficiently accurate integration step.

\section{Beyond independent stationary spins}
\label{sec:03:beyond-independent-stationary-spins}
\subsection{Bloch--Torrey transport and diffusion}
Magnetization density obeys a continuity equation.
With advective velocity $\vect v$ and Fickian diffusion tensor $\mat D$,
the transport contribution is
$-\nabla\cdot(\vect v\vect M)+\nabla\cdot(\mat D\nabla\vect M)$,
applied componentwise. For incompressible flow and constant isotropic $D$,
\begin{equation}
 \partial_t\vect M+\vect v\cdot\nabla\vect M
 =\gamma\vect M\times\vect B-\mat R(\vect M-M_0\vect e_z)
      +D\nabla^2\vect M ,
 \label{eq:03:torrey}
\end{equation}
where $\mat R=\diag(R_2,R_2,R_1)$.
These diffusion terms generalize Bloch dynamics \cite{torrey1956}.
Their boundary conditions matter: reflecting walls impose zero normal
diffusive flux, whereas absorbing or permeable boundaries give different
signals.

For a spatial transverse harmonic $\ee^{-\ii\vect q\cdot\vect r}$,
the Laplacian gives $-q^2$ times the harmonic, so diffusion attenuates
it at rate $Dq^2$. With a time-varying angular wavevector,
the attenuation is $\exp[-D\int q(t)^2\dd t]$ in the free-diffusion,
linear-gradient model. The units $Dq^2$ are inverse seconds.
This connects phase gradients to diffusion sensitivity without
identifying reversible static dephasing with diffusion loss.

\subsection{Bloch--McConnell exchange structure}
For two pools with common volume convention, exchange adds
\[
 \dot{\vect M}_a\big|_{\rm ex}=-k_{ab}\vect M_a+k_{ba}\vect M_b,\qquad
 \dot{\vect M}_b\big|_{\rm ex}=+k_{ab}\vect M_a-k_{ba}\vect M_b .
\]
The rates have units of inverse time, and exchange conserves total
magnetization instantaneously. At equilibrium detailed population
balance requires $k_{ab}M_{0a}=k_{ba}M_{0b}$.
Each pool also has its own Bloch generator and possibly offset.
The resulting coupled affine system can be propagated as a larger matrix.
Its measured decay rates are eigenmodes of the coupled system, not
necessarily either pool's intrinsic $R_1$ or $R_2$
\cite{mcconnell1958}.

\subsection{Where phenomenology requires microscopic input}
Relaxation parameters summarize the spectrum of fluctuating interactions;
exchange parameters summarize transport or chemical kinetics.
Neither can be inferred uniquely from one decay curve without a model.
The same apparent exponential may result from homogeneous relaxation,
fast exchange, or a restricted observation interval of a multiexponential.
Sequence variation, field dependence and preparation dependence provide
additional constraints. The role of a more detailed model is to predict
those changes, not merely to fit more coefficients.

\section{Worked examples}
\label{sec:03:worked-examples}
\subsection{Derivation and quantitative calculation}
\begin{workedexample}[Recovery and an imperfect inversion null]
Let $T_1=\SI{1.2}{\second}$. Saturation recovery at
$t=\SI{0.6}{\second}$ gives $M_z/M_0=1-\ee^{-0.5}=0.3935$.
Perfect inversion nulls at \SI{0.8318}{\second}.
If the initial value is $-0.9M_0$, the null instead occurs at
$1.2\ln1.9=\SI{0.7702}{\second}$. Using the perfect-inversion formula
to infer $T_1$ would underestimate it.
\end{workedexample}
\begin{workedexample}[An FID and a refocused echo]
Let $T_2=\SI{80}{\milli\second}$ and Gaussian static offset standard
deviation $\sigma_f=\SI{20}{\hertz}$, so $\sigma_\omega=2\pi\sigma_f$.
At \SI{20}{\milli\second}, the FID envelope is
$\exp[-0.02/0.08-(2\pi20\cdot0.02)^2/2]=0.0331$.
An ideal echo at \SI{40}{\milli\second} has amplitude
$\exp(-0.04/0.08)=0.6065$.
Signal can therefore increase between the dephased interval and the echo
without any reversal of $T_2$ relaxation.
\end{workedexample}
\subsection{Assumptions, limiting cases and scanner interpretation}
\begin{workedexample}[Continuous-wave saturation]
Take $T_1=\SI{1}{\second}$, $T_2=\SI{0.1}{\second}$ and
$\omega_1=\SI{10}{\radian\per\second}$.
On resonance $\omega_1^2T_1T_2=10$ and
$M_z^{\rm ss}/M_0=M_y^{\rm ss}/M_0=1/11$.
At $\Delta\omega=\SI{100}{\radian\per\second}$,
the denominator is 111:
$M_z/M_0=101/111$, $M_y/M_0=1/111$ and $M_x/M_0=10/111$.
The dispersive and absorptive components differ; a magnitude-only
measurement suppresses their sign information.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Challenge problems}
\label{sec:03:challenge-problems}
\subsection{Derivation and quantitative design}
\begin{challengeproblem}[Repeated pulses and a fixed point]
\label{prob:03:steady}
Identical instantaneous flips $\alpha$ are separated by TR.
Assume all transverse magnetization is spoiled before the next pulse.
Derive the recurrence for pre-pulse $M_z$, its steady state and transient
after $n$ repetitions. Derive the flip angle maximizing immediate
transverse signal, and state when that optimization does not maximize CNR.
\end{challengeproblem}
\begin{challengeproblem}[An echo with changing offsets]
\label{prob:03:echo}
An isochromat has offset $\delta_1$ for $0<t<\tau$ and $\delta_2$
for $\tau<t<2\tau$. Derive its echo after a phase-$\phi$ ideal
$\pi$ pulse. If $\delta_1,\delta_2$ are jointly zero-mean Gaussian
with variance $\sigma_\omega^2$ and correlation $c$, find ensemble
echo attenuation and analyze $c=1,0,-1$.
\end{challengeproblem}
\begin{challengeproblem}[An exchange-conserving mode]
\label{prob:03:exchange}
Two equal pools have transverse rate $R_2$, identical offsets and
symmetric exchange rate $k$. Diagonalize their transverse evolution.
Which modes and rates are measured by equal receive weighting?
What changes if their initial transverse phases differ by $\pi$?
\end{challengeproblem}
\subsection{Model criticism and integrated reasoning}
\begin{challengeproblem}[A fitted T2-star is acquisition-dependent]
\label{prob:03:fit}
For $S(t)=S_0\exp[-R_2t-\sigma_\omega^2t^2/2]$,
derive the rate obtained from the line through two log-signal samples
at $t_1,t_2$. Given rates \SI{40}{\per\second} from $(5,15)$ ms
and \SI{60}{\per\second} from $(15,25)$ ms, infer $R_2$ and
$\sigma_\omega$. Predict an ideal echo at 40 ms.
\end{challengeproblem}
\begin{challengeproblem}[A simulator that creates magnetization]
\label{prob:03:euler}
Quantify the magnitude after $N$ Euler steps of free precession with
step $h$ and total time $t=Nh$. Find its small-$h$ relative error.
Compare with an exact rotation and explain why applying relaxation
after each Euler step can hide, but not repair, the error.
\end{challengeproblem}
