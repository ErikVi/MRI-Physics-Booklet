\chapter{Mathematical and Physical Foundations}
\label{ch:01}

MRI combines a dynamical system with an inverse problem. Fields drive nuclear
magnetic moments; a receiver records noisy linear functionals of the resulting
magnetization. Recovering an image or a material parameter requires both a
physical forward model and an account of which features that model makes
observable. This chapter develops the mathematics needed to make those
statements precise. The spatial transform convention is that of
\hyperref[front:conventions]{front-matter conventions}; changing it within a calculation changes the
inferred encoding direction.

\section{Complex representation and dimensional reasoning}
\label{sec:01:complex-representation-and-dimensional-reasoning}
\subsection{Complex numbers, Euler's formula and phasors}
A complex number $z=x+\ii y$ represents two real components, with
$z^*=x-\ii y$, $\abs z^2=zz^*=x^2+y^2$, and
$z=\abs z\,\ee^{\ii\phi}$. Euler's identity follows by separating the even
and odd powers in the exponential series. Multiplication by
$\ee^{\ii\theta}$ is the planar rotation
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}x'\\y'\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
                  \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Thus $M_x+\ii M_y$ represents a real vector, not an additional physical
magnetization. Conjugation reverses its transverse angle and will be the
algebraic signature of an ideal refocusing pulse.

A real sinusoid is
$v(t)=\operatorname{Re}\{V\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\}$, where the complex
amplitude $V$ stores amplitude and phase. This real signal contains both
positive and negative Fourier frequencies. A complex quadrature measurement
retains two independent channels and distinguishes the directions of
rotation. Taking a real part too early loses this information:
$\cos\omega t$ does not determine whether the underlying transverse
vector rotates clockwise or counterclockwise.

\subsection{Frequency, angular frequency and phase}
Frequency $f$ counts cycles per second; angular frequency
$\omega=2\pi f$ measures radians per second. A time-dependent phase obeys
$\phi(t)-\phi(t_0)=\int_{t_0}^t\omega(\tau)\dd\tau$ if $\omega$ is
defined as its signed rate. In this book $\omega_0=\gamma B_0$
is a positive Larmor \emph{magnitude}, while the phase rate of $M_+$ is
$-\omega_0$. These uses must not be conflated.

In $\ee^{-\ii2\pi kx}$, $k$ is in cycles per metre. A wavevector in
radians per metre instead occurs as $\ee^{-\ii qx}$, with $q=2\pi k$.
A numerical library cannot detect an interchange: both variables are
floating-point numbers, and radians are dimensionless in SI algebra.
The sign and cyclic/angular convention are part of a variable's definition.

\subsection{SI units, scaling and dimensional checks}
A magnetic moment has units $\si{\ampere\square\metre}$, energy in a field
is in joules, and magnetization is moment per volume,
$\si{\ampere\per\metre}$. Magnetic flux density $B$ is in tesla, whereas
field strength $H$ is in $\si{\ampere\per\metre}$.
They are not interchangeable in a torque or Zeeman-energy formula.
For a linear isotropic material $B=\mu H$ is a constitutive approximation.

Exponents, trigonometric arguments and logarithmic arguments must be
dimensionless. For example, $\gamma Gxt$ is dimensionless because
$\gamma$ has units $\si{\per\second\per\tesla}$ and $G$ has units
$\si{\tesla\per\metre}$. Such checks reject $G/t$ where an integrated
gradient $Gt$ is required, but not a missing $2\pi$, a wrong sign,
or a factor of two in complex-noise variance. Those require independent
physical and limiting-case checks.

\section{Linear algebra for spin and encoding}
\label{sec:01:linear-algebra-for-spin-and-encoding}
\subsection{Vector spaces, inner products and adjoints}
For complex columns, define
$\langle\vect x,\vect y\rangle=\vect x\adj\vect y$ and
$\norm{\vect x}_2^2=\vect x\adj\vect x$. The adjoint is the
conjugate transpose, characterized by
$\langle\mat A\vect x,\vect y\rangle
=\langle\vect x,\mat A\adj\vect y\rangle$.
This identity defines continuous-operator adjoints too. For functions,
the inner product is $\int_\Omega f^*g\dd\vect r$;
discrete quadrature weights must be included when approximating it.

A Hermitian matrix satisfies $\mat H=\mat H\adj$ and has real quadratic
forms $\vect x\adj\mat H\vect x$. A positive-definite covariance
$\mat\Psi$ induces
$\norm{\vect y}_{\mat\Psi^{-1}}^2=\vect y\adj\mat\Psi^{-1}\vect y$.
If $\mat L\mat L\adj=\mat\Psi$, whitening by $\mat L^{-1}$ turns
this into a Euclidean norm. The encoding matrix and the measurements
must both be whitened; changing only the data changes the model.

\subsection{Eigenvalues, eigenvectors and matrix exponentials}
If $\mat A\vect v_j=\lambda_j\vect v_j$ and the eigenvectors span the space,
$\mat A=\mat V\mat\Lambda\mat V^{-1}$, so
$\ee^{t\mat A}=\mat V\diag(\ee^{t\lambda_j})\mat V^{-1}$ solves
$\dot{\vect x}=\mat A\vect x$.
The series $\ee^{\mat A}=\sum_{n\geq0}\mat A^n/n!$ remains valid
when $\mat A$ is not diagonalizable. A Jordan block produces polynomial
factors multiplying exponentials; diagonalization is not a universal
numerical method.

A Hermitian generator $\mat H$ gives a unitary propagator
$\mat U=\ee^{-\ii t\mat H/\hbar}$ with $\mat U\adj\mat U=\mat I$.
A real skew-symmetric generator gives an orthogonal rotation.
Dissipative generators instead have decaying modes. These distinctions
separate coherent spin evolution from relaxation.

Time-dependent generators require ordering.
$\ee^{\Delta t\mat A_2}\ee^{\Delta t\mat A_1}$ generally differs from
$\ee^{\Delta t(\mat A_1+\mat A_2)}$.
Series expansion shows a leading difference
$\tfrac12\Delta t^2[\mat A_2,\mat A_1]$.
Finite rotations about different RF axes therefore cannot be combined
by merely adding scalar flip angles.

\subsection{Singular values, rank and conditioning}
Every finite matrix has an SVD
$\mat A=\mat U\mat\Sigma\mat V\adj$, with orthonormal singular vectors
and nonnegative singular values $\sigma_j(\mat A)$.
The argument distinguishes them from the noise standard deviation $\sigma$.
For $\vect y=\mat A\vect x+\vect n$,
\[
 \vect u_j\adj\vect y=\sigma_j(\mat A)\vect v_j\adj\vect x+
                         \vect u_j\adj\vect n .
\]
Inversion divides noise and signal by the same singular value.
Zero values imply nonidentifiability; small values imply instability.
For full column rank,
$\kappa_2(\mat A)=\sigma_{\max}(\mat A)/\sigma_{\min}(\mat A)$
measures worst-case sensitivity. Repeating nearly identical encoding
does not necessarily improve conditioning.

The minimum-norm least-squares solution is
$\mat A^+\vect y=\sum_{\sigma_j>0}
\vect v_j(\vect u_j\adj\vect y)/\sigma_j$.
Discarding small singular values stabilizes it by also removing object
components. The result has a resolution operator rather than an exact
inverse, an essential point for later parallel imaging and regularization.

\section{Fourier analysis and linear systems}
\label{sec:01:fourier-analysis-and-linear-systems}
\subsection{Transform pairs and distributions}
For an integrable one-dimensional function,
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde f(k)=\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)\ee^{-\ii2\pi kx}\dd x .
 \label{eq:01:ft}
\end{equation}
Exponentials are eigenfunctions of translations; their coefficients
describe spatial variation at each frequency. If $x$ has units of length,
$\widetilde f$ has units of $f$ times length.

For $f(x)=\exp[-x^2/(2a^2)]$, integration by parts gives
$\widetilde f'(k)=-4\pi^2a^2k\widetilde f(k)$.
Using $\widetilde f(0)=\sqrt{2\pi}a$ gives the pair
\begin{equation}
 \ee^{-x^2/(2a^2)}
 \ \longleftrightarrow\
 \sqrt{2\pi}a\,\ee^{-2\pi^2a^2k^2},\qquad a>0 .
 \label{eq:01:gaussian}
\end{equation}
A unit-area Gaussian of width $\epsilon$ approaches the Dirac delta
distribution: integration against a continuous test function approaches
that function's value at zero. A delta is defined through its action,
$\int\delta(x-x_0)g(x)\dd x=g(x_0)$, not as an ordinary infinitely
high function. Change of variables gives $\delta(ax)=\delta(x)/\abs a$.

Insert \cref{eq:01:ft} into a Gaussian-regularized inverse:
\[
 \int\widetilde f(k)\ee^{+\ii2\pi kx}
        \ee^{-2\pi^2\epsilon^2k^2}\dd k
 =\int f(u)\frac{\ee^{-(x-u)^2/(2\epsilon^2)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\epsilon}\dd u .
\]
Absolute integrability justifies interchanging the regularized integrals.
Taking $\epsilon\to0$ at continuity points proves inversion.
A symmetric limit at a jump gives the midpoint. The transform extends
to square-integrable functions by completion, with mean-square equality.
Substituting the inverse for $g$ and recognizing $\widetilde f^*$
also gives Parseval--Plancherel:
\begin{equation}
 \int f^*(x)g(x)\dd x=\int\widetilde f^*(k)\widetilde g(k)\dd k .
 \label{eq:01:parseval}
\end{equation}
Squared norm is conserved, with dimensions carried by the integration
measures. Fourier inversion does not mean pointwise convergence under
every possible integrability assumption.

For $\operatorname{rect}(x/L)=1$ on $\abs x<L/2$, zero elsewhere,
direct integration gives
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{rect}(x/L)\longleftrightarrow L\sinc(kL).
 \label{eq:01:rect}
\end{equation}
Substitution further yields
$f(x-x_0)\leftrightarrow\ee^{-\ii2\pi kx_0}\widetilde f(k)$ and
$\ee^{+\ii2\pi k_0x}f(x)\leftrightarrow\widetilde f(k-k_0)$.
Integration by parts yields
$f'(x)\leftrightarrow\ii2\pi k\widetilde f(k)$ when boundary terms vanish.
Phase ramps and displacement are therefore Fourier conjugates.

\subsection{Convolution, filtering and impulse responses}
For a linear shift-invariant operator, write
$f(x)=\int f(u)\delta(x-u)\dd u$ and apply linearity and shift invariance:
\[
 (\mathcal H f)(x)=(h*f)(x)=\int h(x-u)f(u)\dd u .
\]
Here $h$ is the response to a delta input. Transforming this integral
and changing variables proves $\FT(h*f)=\widetilde h\,\widetilde f$.
The dual identity turns multiplication into convolution.
A finite frequency acquisition window therefore convolves the image
with a point-spread function (PSF). Coil sensitivity instead multiplies
the object and convolves its spectrum.

Linearity and shift invariance are separate assumptions.
Spatially varying off-resonance can preserve linearity in the object
while breaking shift invariance; it then requires a kernel $h(x,u)$,
not $h(x-u)$. A single PSF does not characterize such an entire image.

\subsection{Sampling, Nyquist limits and discrete transforms}
Sampling at spacing $\Delta x$ multiplies $f$ by a comb:
$f_s(x)=\sum_n f(n\Delta x)\delta(x-n\Delta x)$.
Poisson summation gives, in a distributional sense,
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde f_s(k)=\frac{1}{\Delta x}
       \sum_{\ell\in\mathbb Z}\widetilde f(k-\ell/\Delta x).
 \label{eq:01:replicas}
\end{equation}
If $\widetilde f(k)=0$ for $\abs k\geq K$ and $1/\Delta x>2K$,
replicas do not overlap. Selecting and scaling the central replica
before inversion gives
\begin{equation}
 f(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z} f(n\Delta x)
       \sinc\left(\frac{x-n\Delta x}{\Delta x}\right).
 \label{eq:01:shannon}
\end{equation}
Strict inequality avoids a populated band-edge ambiguity.
This theorem requires infinitely many exact samples and band limitation.
Finite MRI acquisitions approximate those hypotheses in different domains.
Shannon's original treatment establishes the sampling framework
\cite{shannon1949}.

The finite DFT is an exact basis change on $\mathbb C^N$:
\begin{equation}
 X_p=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x_n\ee^{-\ii2\pi pn/N},\qquad
 x_n=\frac1N\sum_{p=0}^{N-1}X_p\ee^{+\ii2\pi pn/N}.
 \label{eq:01:dft}
\end{equation}
Substitution reduces the inverse to a geometric sum: it is $N$ for
equal indices modulo $N$, zero otherwise.
Thus $\mat F\adj\mat F=N\mat I$ and
$\sum_n\abs{x_n}^2=N^{-1}\sum_p\abs{X_p}^2$.
An FFT is an algorithm, not a different transform.

Continuous interpretation introduces periodic extension over $N\Delta x$.
Products of DFTs represent circular convolution. Padding enough to contain
the support of a desired linear convolution prevents wraparound, whereas
padding measured frequency data does not restore absent frequencies.
Unshifted indices above $N/2$ represent negative frequencies.
For even $N$ the Nyquist bin is one periodic boundary bin, not two
independent observations.

\section{Probability and measurement noise}
\label{sec:01:probability-and-measurement-noise}
\subsection{Random variables, expectations and covariance}
For mean $\vect\mu=\E[\vect n]$,
$\mat\Psi=\E[(\vect n-\vect\mu)(\vect n-\vect\mu)\adj]$.
It is Hermitian positive semidefinite since
$\vect a\adj\mat\Psi\vect a=\E[\abs{\vect a\adj(\vect n-\vect\mu)}^2]\geq0$.
A linear operation gives the exact identity
$\Cov(\mat L\vect n)=\mat L\mat\Psi\mat L\adj$,
regardless of the noise distribution. Zero covariance implies independence
for jointly Gaussian real variables, but not in general.

Complex noise also has pseudo-covariance
$\E[(\vect n-\vect\mu)(\vect n-\vect\mu)^{\mathsf T}]$.
Proper noise has zero pseudo-covariance.
Circular Gaussian noise is invariant under common phase rotation.
Receiver filtering and channel coupling determine whether the usual
independent proper-noise approximation is justified.

\subsection{Gaussian complex noise and magnitude statistics}
If $n=n_R+\ii n_I$, with independent
$n_R,n_I\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$, then
\[
 \psi=\E[\abs n^2]=2\sigma^2,\qquad
 p(n)=\frac{1}{\pi\psi}\exp(-\abs n^2/\psi).
\]
For noiseless amplitude $A\geq0$, express $y=A+n=r\ee^{\ii\varphi}$.
The polar-coordinate Jacobian is $r$. Integrating over angle gives
\begin{equation}
 p(r\mid A)=\frac r{\sigma^2}
   \exp\left[-\frac{r^2+A^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]
   I_0\left(\frac{rA}{\sigma^2}\right),\quad r\geq0,
 \label{eq:01:rice}
\end{equation}
where $I_0(z)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_0^{2\pi}\ee^{z\cos\theta}\dd\theta$.
This is Rician noise. At $A=0$, the Rayleigh limit has
$\E[r]=\sigma\sqrt{\pi/2}$ and $\E[r^2]=2\sigma^2$;
magnitude background remains positive \cite{gudbjartsson1995}.
At high SNR,
$r\approx A+n_R+n_I^2/(2A)$, giving bias $\sigma^2/(2A)$.
That expansion is invalid near zero signal.

\subsection{Likelihood, bias and propagation of uncertainty}
Likelihood treats observed data as fixed and model parameters as unknown.
It is not a normalized parameter density without a prior.
For a differentiable estimator, linearization gives
$\Cov(g)\approx\mat J_g\Cov(\vect y)\mat J_g\adj$,
using real coordinates for nonlinear complex operations.
Small fluctuations relative to the Jacobian's variation scale are required.
Phase is singular near a signal zero, invalidating this approximation.

Bias is $\E[\widehat\theta]-\theta$, and variance measures scatter about
the estimator mean. Scalar mean-squared error is variance plus squared bias.
Logging a magnitude decay changes both the noise distribution and its
weighting; it is not maximum likelihood for complex Gaussian data.

\section{Estimation and experimental information}
\label{sec:01:estimation-and-experimental-information}
\subsection{Least squares and maximum likelihood}
For $\vect y=\mat A\vect x+\vect n$ with proper Gaussian noise and known,
parameter-independent $\mat\Psi>0$, maximizing likelihood minimizes
$Q=(\vect y-\mat A\vect x)\adj\mat\Psi^{-1}
(\vect y-\mat A\vect x)$.
Setting real and imaginary derivatives to zero, equivalently making the
weighted residual orthogonal to all encoding columns, yields
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
 \widehat{\vect x}&=(\mat A\adj\mat\Psi^{-1}\mat A)^{-1}
                    \mat A\adj\mat\Psi^{-1}\vect y,\\
 \Cov(\widehat{\vect x})&=(\mat A\adj\mat\Psi^{-1}\mat A)^{-1}.
 \end{split}
 \label{eq:01:wls}
\end{equation}
Full column rank is required. The covariance follows by linear propagation.
A pseudoinverse in a rank-deficient system chooses a solution but cannot
make a null-space component observable.

\subsection{Fisher information and the Cramer--Rao bound}
For real parameters $\vect\theta$ and complex mean $\vect\mu(\vect\theta)$,
differentiate the Gaussian log likelihood to obtain score
$u_a=2\operatorname{Re}\{(\partial_a\vect\mu)\adj
\mat\Psi^{-1}(\vect y-\vect\mu)\}$.
Proper-noise covariance gives the Fisher matrix
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I_{ab}
 =2\operatorname{Re}\{(\partial_a\vect\mu)\adj
                         \mat\Psi^{-1}(\partial_b\vect\mu)\}.
 \label{eq:01:fisher}
\end{equation}
A parameter-dependent covariance requires additional derivative terms.

For a regular likelihood and unbiased scalar estimator,
$\E[u]=0$ and differentiation under the integral gives
$\E[(\widehat\theta-\theta)u]=1$.
Cauchy--Schwarz then gives
$\Var(\widehat\theta)\E[u^2]\geq1$.
In vector form,
$\Cov(\widehat{\vect\theta})\succeq\mat{\mathcal I}^{-1}$.
This is a local lower bound under regularity and unbiasedness, not a
guarantee of finite-sample attainability. Constraints, bias and
multiple solutions must be examined separately.

\subsection{Identifiability and nuisance parameters}
Two parameter sets generating identical noiseless data cannot be
distinguished without added information. Independent sensitivity directions
are needed for local identifiability. Global identifiability is stronger:
phase wrapping can give multiple solutions despite a nonzero local derivative.

Partition parameters into target $\theta$ and nuisance vector $\vect\eta$.
Block inversion gives effective information
\[
 \mathcal I_{\mathrm{eff}}=\mathcal I_{\theta\theta}
 -\mat{\mathcal I}_{\theta\eta}\mat{\mathcal I}_{\eta\eta}^{-1}
  \mat{\mathcal I}_{\eta\theta}.
\]
The subtracted term is nonnegative. Treating unknown receive amplitude,
transmit calibration or baseline as known overstates precision.
Useful contrast must create sensitivity distinguishable from those
nuisance effects.

\section{Fields, dipoles and angular momentum}
\label{sec:01:fields-dipoles-and-angular-momentum}
\subsection{Maxwell equations and quasistatic limits}
In macroscopic SI notation,
\[
 \nabla\cdot\vect B=0,\quad \nabla\times\vect E=-\partial_t\vect B,\quad
 \nabla\cdot\vect D_{\rm el}=\rho_{\rm free},\quad
 \nabla\times\vect H=\vect J_{\rm free}+\partial_t\vect D_{\rm el}.
\]
Electric displacement $\vect D_{\rm el}$ is not the diffusion tensor.
Constitutive relations such as $\vect D_{\rm el}=\epsilon\vect E$ and
$\vect J=\sigma_{\rm el}\vect E$ require material assumptions.
Gradient fields are commonly treated magnetoquasistatically.
Faraday induction still produces electric fields and cannot be discarded.
RF wavelengths in tissue may be comparable to body dimensions; a uniform
RF field is consequently a local or idealized model, not a universal fact.

$B_z=B_0+G_xx$ describes only the leading longitudinal component.
Other field components must satisfy Maxwell's equations in a current-free
region. Dropping their higher-order contribution to the field magnitude
is an approximation, not a complete magnetic-field solution.

\subsection{Magnetic dipole energy and torque}
A fixed magnetic dipole has $U=-\vect\mu\cdot\vect B$.
Under a small active rotation $\delta\vect\theta$,
$\delta\vect\mu=\delta\vect\theta\times\vect\mu$, so
$\delta U=-\delta\vect\theta\cdot(\vect\mu\times\vect B)$.
Thus $\vect\tau=\vect\mu\times\vect B$.
A fixed orientation in a nonuniform field also experiences
$\vect F=\nabla(\vect\mu\cdot\vect B)$ in the dipole approximation.
Torque, translational force and dissipative heating are distinct mechanisms.

For angular momentum $\vect J$ with $\vect\mu=\gamma\vect J$,
$\dot{\vect\mu}=\gamma\vect\mu\times\vect B$.
Consequently $\dd(\vect\mu^2)/\dd t=0$: torque causes precession,
not spontaneous alignment. Equilibration needs environmental coupling.

\subsection{Angular momentum and rotating coordinates}
For axes rotating at angular velocity $\vect\Omega$,
\[
 \left(\frac{\dd\vect v}{\dd t}\right)_{\rm lab}
 =\left(\frac{\dd\vect v}{\dd t}\right)_{\rm rot}
       +\vect\Omega\times\vect v .
\]
Differentiate $\vect v=\sum_jv'_j\vect e'_j$ and use
$\dot{\vect e}'_j=\vect\Omega\times\vect e'_j$ to obtain this result.
Magnetic precession in rotating coordinates is therefore
$\dot{\vect M}_{\rm rot}=\gamma\vect M\times
(\vect B+\vect\Omega/\gamma)$.
Resonant proton-frame axes have
$\vect\Omega=-\omega_{\rm RF}\vect e_z$; their apparent longitudinal
field is reduced by $\omega_{\rm RF}/\gamma$.
\Cref{ch:04} derives the same result from the quantum propagator.

\section{Worked examples}
\label{sec:01:worked-examples}
\subsection{Derivation and quantitative calculation}
\begin{workedexample}[A sampled phase is ambiguous]
For $s_n=\ee^{-\ii2\pi f n\Delta t}$ and
$\Delta t=\SI{10}{\micro\second}$, the sample rate is
$\SI{100}{\kilo\hertz}$.
Offsets $f=\SI{63}{\kilo\hertz}$ and $f'=-\SI{37}{\kilo\hertz}$
give identical samples since their phase difference is $-2\pi n$.
A receiver with physical-offset band $[-50,50)$ kHz reports the latter.
More samples at fixed dwell improve frequency-grid spacing, not the
unaliased band.
\end{workedexample}
\begin{workedexample}[Averaging correlated channels]
Two measurements of a common complex mean have real-quadrature standard
deviation $\sigma=2$ and complex covariance
$\mat\Psi=8\begin{pmatrix}1&0.3\\0.3&1\end{pmatrix}$.
Their mean has complex variance
$\tfrac14(8+8+2\cdot2.4)=5.2$, or real-quadrature standard deviation
$\sqrt{2.6}=1.612$. Independent channels would give $1.414$.
The improvement factor is $1.240$, not $\sqrt2$.
\end{workedexample}
\subsection{Assumptions, limiting cases and scanner interpretation}
\begin{workedexample}[Precision of a known-amplitude decay]
A real observation with mean $A\ee^{-t/T}$ and variance $\sigma^2$
has information
$\mathcal I_{TT}=A^2t^2\ee^{-2t/T}/(\sigma^2T^4)$ when $A$ is known.
The derivative of its log with respect to $t$ is $2/t-2/T$, giving
optimal $t=T$ for one observation with fixed noise.
For $A/\sigma=100$ and $T=\SI{80}{\milli\second}$, the standard-deviation
bound is $T\ee/100=\SI{2.17}{\milli\second}$.
With unknown $A$, one sample identifies neither parameter separately.
This timing also need not optimize precision per unit scan time.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Challenge problems}
\label{sec:01:challenge-problems}
\subsection{Derivation and quantitative design}
\begin{challengeproblem}[Recoverability and aliasing]
\label{prob:01:alias}
For $x_n=n\Delta x$, prove that
$\ee^{\ii2\pi kx}$ and $\ee^{\ii2\pi(k+\ell/\Delta x)x}$
are indistinguishable for integer $\ell$. Construct a nonzero smooth
function whose samples all vanish. Derive the periodic image produced
by sampling $\widetilde f$ at spacing $\Delta k$. What assumption permits
unique recovery, and why does increasing the reconstruction matrix not
supply it?
\end{challengeproblem}
\begin{challengeproblem}[An optimal complex linear estimate]
\label{prob:01:blue}
Let $\vect y=\vect a x+\vect n$ with complex $x$ and
$\Cov(\vect n)=\mat\Psi>0$. Among estimators
$\widehat x=\vect w\adj\vect y$, derive the minimum-variance unbiased
weight and variance. Evaluate them for $\vect a=(1,2)^{\mathsf T}$ and
$\mat\Psi=\psi\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{pmatrix}$,
real $\abs\rho<1$. Compare $\rho=0$ and $1/2$; explain a vanishing weight.
\end{challengeproblem}
\begin{challengeproblem}[A nuisance amplitude in relaxometry]
\label{prob:01:fisher}
Two independent real observations have means $A\ee^{-t_j/T}$ and
variance $\sigma^2$. Derive their Fisher matrix and the information for
$T$ after eliminating unknown $A$. For $t_1=0$, $t_2=t>0$,
derive the equation for optimal $t/T$ when information per pair is the
objective. Explain the singularity if both times are identical.
\end{challengeproblem}
\subsection{Model criticism and integrated reasoning}
\begin{challengeproblem}[The inverse is not the adjoint]
\label{prob:01:adjoint}
For $\mat A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&\epsilon\end{pmatrix}$,
$0<\epsilon\ll1$, compute the inverse, normal matrix and covariance
of the inverse estimate for $\Cov(\vect n)=\psi\mat I$.
Find the leading condition-number scaling. An investigator uses
$\mat A\adj\vect y$ and reports low noise. Find its mean and explain
why low noise does not establish accurate recovery.
\end{challengeproblem}
\begin{challengeproblem}[Energy, torque and rotating signs]
\label{prob:01:torque}
A positive-$\gamma$ dipole begins along $+x$ in $B_0\vect e_z$.
Derive its initial derivative, full trajectory and rotating derivative
for axes with $\vect\Omega=-\omega_{\rm RF}\vect e_z$.
Does torque alone decrease its energy? Identify the missing physics in
the claim that a static field makes all spins spiral into its
lowest-energy orientation.
\end{challengeproblem}
